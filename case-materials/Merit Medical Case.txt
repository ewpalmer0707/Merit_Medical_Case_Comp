This case was prepared as a basis for class discussion rather than to illustrate either effective or ineffective handling of a
business situation. The protagonist and her internal dialogue are dramatized for teaching purposes; facts and figures are drawn

Introduction

In late February 2026, Alisha Jerauld, Merit Medical's Vice President of Environmental, Social, and
Governance (ESG), had two contradictory reports on her desk. The first was a draft press release for
Merit's third Newsweek sustainability recognition in a year.i The second was an internal rollout report
showing that most of Merit's suppliers had not yet completed the sustainability assessment the company
had asked them to fill out. The response rates were thinnest among smaller manufacturers in Mexico and
China, exactly the regions Merit's own research had flagged as having the least developed rules for
tracking emissions and labor practices.ii Merit's growing pile of public awards was starting to invite a
harder question: did the story match the substance behind it? Alisha had six weeks to bring the Board's
Governance and Sustainability Committee a plan that answered both problems at once.


Company Overview

"Merit Medical Systems, Inc. is a leading manufacturer and marketer of proprietary medical devices used
in interventional, diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, particularly in cardiology, radiology, oncology,
critical care and endoscopy. We strive to be the most customer-focused company in healthcare. Each day
we are determined to make a difference by understanding our customers' needs and innovating and
delivering a diverse range of products that improve the lives of people and communities throughout the
world. We believe that long-term value is created for our customers, employees, shareholders, and
communities when we focus outward and are determined to deliver an exceptional customer experience.
Merit Medical Systems, Inc. was founded in 1987 by Fred P. Lampropoulos, Kent W. Stanger, Darla Gill
and William Padilla. Initially, we focused our operations on injection and insert molding of plastics. Our
first product was a specialized control syringe used to inject contrast solution into a patient's arteries for a
diagnostic cardiac procedure called an angiogram. Since that time, our products and product lines have
expanded substantially, both through internal research and development projects and through strategic
acquisitions." —Merit Medical Systems, Inc., "Our Company," 2025 Annual Report

Why Merit Matters in Healthcare

Merit's devices are the tools clinicians use to work inside the body without open surgery. Its portfolio
includes steerable guide wires and diagnostic catheters for navigating blood vessels, drainage catheters,
embolotherapy products that block blood flow to tumors or deliver chemotherapy to liver cancers, and
peritoneal dialysis catheters for home dialysis.iii Merit describes these as disposable devices,iv so hospitals
reorder them continuously. A missing component at one supplier can become a missing device in a
procedure room.​
​
That risk is real across the industry. In a survey by the patient-safety nonprofit ECRI, almost half of
healthcare professionals said drug, supply, or equipment shortages had delayed patient treatment, and
some reported surgeries rescheduled, postponed, or canceled.v The FDA has warned that shortages of
devices including hemodialysis catheters forced clinicians to delay care for children, and noted that U.S.
manufacturers must report supply disruptions only during a declared public health emergency.vi Because a
new supplier can take 9–12 months to qualify (Exhibit 2), Merit's supplier relationships are part of patient
care, not just procurement.​


Merit runs this work with roughly 7,100 associates (Exhibit 1), a sales and clinical support team of more
than 700,vii and manufacturing sites that include its Utah headquarters, Galway (Ireland), Tijuana
(Mexico), and Singapore. Merit calls Tijuana one of its primary manufacturing and assembly facilities.viii

     ●​ A non-GAAP operating margin of 20%–22% in 2026

In 2025, Merit delivered 6.8% organic constant-currency growth, a 20.3% non-GAAP operating margin,
and more than $215 million in free cash flow, leaving 2026 as the program's final year.xiii
Taken together, these goals set the bar for Alisha's plan. It has to serve hospital customers who
increasingly screen suppliers on ESG, live up to a value statement that already promises supplier
standards, protect the supply continuity that patients depend on, and fit within a company that has
publicly committed to margin and cash discipline.


Merit's Sustainability Program

Merit's Board of Directors oversees ESG issues through a Governance and Sustainability Committee,
supported by an internal Corporate Sustainability Council that Alisha chairs.xiv In 2024–2025, an outside
firm ran a structured materiality assessment, surveying employees, suppliers, and customers on which
environmental and social issues mattered most, and identified ten priority topics, including the working
conditions of employees across Merit's value chain and how fairly Merit manages its size advantage over
smaller suppliers.xv Merit has also set a goal of cutting its own direct (Scope 1 and 2) greenhouse gas
emissions 40 percent by 2030 versus a 2020 baseline. Progress has been mixed: emissions rose 2 percent
in 2025 even as renewable electricity use climbed to 27 percent. Merit has not yet finished measuring
Scope 3 emissions — those created by suppliers, shippers, and product disposal, which for most
manufacturers dwarf direct emissions — and only set 2024 as its baseline year for that count.xvi
Problem 1: Engaging a Global, Unequal Supplier Base

Merit's supplier base is large and concentrated: more than 1,030 direct suppliers across 42 countries, with
the top 78 accounting for roughly two-thirds of spend, and only about half of all suppliers based in the
continental U.S.xvii Historically, Merit selected suppliers almost entirely on quality; environmental and
social criteria are newer. To close that gap, Merit began routing suppliers through EcoVadis, a third-party
platform that scores how responsibly a business operates.xviii​


The rollout landed unevenly. Large, often publicly traded suppliers — who already report sustainability
data to their own customers — generally finished quickly. Many smaller and mid-sized suppliers,
especially precision-parts makers and contract manufacturers in Mexico and China, had no dedicated
sustainability staff, no emissions-tracking system, and sometimes no English-language documentation of
practices they may already follow. For a supplier like this, an EcoVadis request can look less like
partnership and more like unfunded homework — the exact risk Merit's own materiality assessment
flagged under "management of supplier relationships."xix The problem is compounded by geography:
Merit's own climate research already identified Mexico and the China/Singapore region as facing the
highest risk on this exact issue, because local rules pushing suppliers toward better reporting remain
comparatively underdeveloped there.xx​


This is not simply a matter of Merit's preference: Merit is itself subject to corporate social responsibility
(CSR) laws, including the California Transparency in Supply Chains Act and the UK Modern Slavery
Act, that require it to monitor labor standards across its own supply chain — so a supplier's sustainability
responsiveness has become a compliance question for Merit, not only a reputational one.xxi
Exhibit 3 sketches a way to sort suppliers by leverage (spend concentration, sole-source status) and by
readiness (size, existing reporting habits, local regulation). "Anchor Partners" — large, sophisticated,
already reporting — sit at one end. Small, single-plant manufacturers in Mexico and China, which carry
Merit's biggest data and continuity blind spot, sit at the other. Merit's options range from pushing the same
EcoVadis requirement on everyone (fast, but risks relationship strain or shallow answers), to narrowing
focus to the top 78 suppliers (efficient, but leaves the highest-risk suppliers untouched for years), to a
differentiated approach that offers smaller suppliers real support — and shows them what they personally
gain — before holding them to the same bar as Merit's largest partners.


Problem 2: Communicating ESG Performance Without Overreaching

Merit's 2025 awards were real and independently earned — three Newsweek recognitions and a Gallup
Exceptional Workplace Award following a record 93 percent employee survey participation rate.xxii But
some of Merit's own numbers still have gaps: Scope 3 is unmeasured, direct emissions rose 2 percent, and
the workplace injury rate ticked up slightly.xxiii Spotlighting the awards while glossing over the harder
numbers risks a credibility problem the moment someone looks past the press release.
Regulatory ground is also shifting. Merit's sustainability reporting is modeled on the EU's Corporate
Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), but in December 2025 EU lawmakers sharply narrowed
which companies CSRD applies to and delayed deadlines for many mid-sized firms by two to three
years.xxiv Separately, the EU's proposed Green Claims Directive — which would have required proof
behind claims like "sustainable" — stalled in 2025, but a related, already-binding law will make vague or
offset-based "climate neutral" claims illegal starting September 27, 2026.xxv For a company collecting
sustainability awards, the message is clear: scrutiny of corporate environmental claims is not going away,
even as the specific rules keep moving.​


The EU's regulatory reach compounds this problem in a way that goes beyond climate-claims wording.
Medical devices sold in the EU already require CE marking under the EU Medical Device Regulation
(Regulation (EU) 2017/745, MDR), and Merit's own 2025 Annual Report acknowledges that, given the
cost and complexity of that certification, the company will in some cases discontinue or postpone a
product's EU introduction rather than pursue approval under the newer rules.xxvi That trade-off shows up
concretely with battery-powered products: any device that incorporates a battery must now also satisfy the
EU's separate Batteries Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2023/1542), which layers its own CE marking,
technical documentation, and — starting in 2027 — end-user battery-removability design rules on top of
MDR, on a compliance track that runs largely independent of the device's own approval.xxvii Faced with
that, Merit has chosen in at least one product line to sell only the non-battery version of a device in
Europe — even though the battery-enabled version is more advanced and more accurate — simply to
avoid stacking two overlapping, still-maturing regulatory regimes onto a single product. The same
dynamic shows up elsewhere: Merit's own filings flag active EU consideration of restrictions on PFAS
materials and on Ethylene Oxide sterilization, both of which the company currently relies on to
manufacture and sterilize its products, alongside a patchwork of country-by-country packaging and
extended-producer-responsibility rules.xxviii Each new rule brings its own declaration of conformity,
documentation, and internal sign-off, so Merit's EU compliance effort looks less like managing a fixed
checklist and more like continuously re-configuring for a moving target — and that recurring compliance
cost eventually has to be recovered somewhere, typically in product price, which cuts against the same
cost discipline that anchors Merit's broader strategy.​


At the same time, silence has a cost: hospital systems increasingly screen suppliers on ESG performance,
some of Merit's own manufacturing customers are themselves affected by CSRD, and Merit's recruiting
data suggests its employee-friendly reputation is becoming a real hiring advantage in competitive labor
markets. The task is not whether to tell Merit's story, but how to tell it so it builds trust with investors,
hospital customers, employees, and the suppliers now being asked to open their own books.


The Question Before Alisha

Alisha has six weeks before she presents to the Board's Governance and Sustainability Committee. She
needs a supply chain sustainability strategy the Committee can approve and Merit can start piloting this
year.​


Merit's top goal: build a credible, verifiable picture of sustainability performance across its supply base,
one Merit can act on and defend publicly, while strengthening the supplier relationships that keep devices
reaching patients.​


The stakeholders who matter most to that goal:
   1.​ Suppliers, especially the high-power, low-readiness manufacturers in Mexico and China (Exhibit
         3)
   2.​ Hospital and health-system customers, who increasingly screen suppliers on ESG performance
   3.​ The Board and investors, who will weigh any plan against the Continued Growth Initiatives
         targets
   4.​ Merit associates, especially the procurement and quality teams who manage supplier relationships
         every day​


Two common strategies are summarized in Exhibit 5: (A) require every supplier to complete EcoVadis
on a fixed deadline, or (B) focus first on the 78 suppliers that hold about two-thirds of spend. Merit is
open to either, a combination, or a completely different approach, as long as your proposal makes these
five things clear:
   1.​ Who Merit engages first, and why, and what waits until later
   2.​ What Merit asks of suppliers, and what suppliers get in return
   3.​ What it costs, who pays, and how it fits Merit's margin and cash targets
   4.​ How Merit will know it's working: metrics, targets, and a timeline
   5.​ What Merit will say publicly, to whom, and how it avoids claiming more than its data supports

   What supplier sustainability strategy should Merit adopt to build credible, verifiable sustainability data

                          reflects Merit's values and fits its financial commitments?

  2.​ Improve the coverage and quality of Merit's supplier sustainability data without disrupting supply

  3.​ Reflect Merit's vision, mission, and HEART values, and fit within its Continued Growth Initiatives

  4.​ Answer the following three questions:
            a. Where should Merit focus first, and why? (Consider Exhibits 2 and 3.)
            b. How does your strategy build trust and competitive advantage with the four stakeholder

            c. What does your strategy cost, what does it return, and how will Merit measure success?​


Teams from every major are encouraged. You may lead with an operations, finance, marketing and
communications, strategy, information systems, public policy, global studies, or other lens. Whatever your
angle, your proposal must still work within the supply chain realities described in this case.

Evaluation Criteria (in order of importance)

      ●​ Strategic rationale and fit with Merit's goals: a clear, defended choice that advances Merit's top
          goal and reflects its vision, mission, HEART values, and financial commitments.
      ●​ Feasibility and scalability: realistic strategies Merit could start this year and extend across a
          global supplier base, operationally and financially.
      ●​ Quantitative rigor: recommendations backed by defensible data analysis, with costs, returns, and
          measurable targets.
      ●​ Effective social impact: improves outcomes for suppliers, their workers, and patients while
          avoiding unintended harm, such as pushing out small suppliers or overstating progress.
      ●​ Operational considerations: detailed pilot design, process, owners, rollout timeline, and risk
          mitigation.
      ●​ Creativity and innovation: novel, compelling ideas that align with Merit's mission.
