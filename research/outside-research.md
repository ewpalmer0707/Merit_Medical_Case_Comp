## 2. EcoVadis: how it works, cost, burden, alternatives

### 2.1 Methodology (EcoVadis public disclosure document, 2026)
Source: https://ecovadis.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/EcoVadis-Sustainability-Rating-Methodology-disclosure-document-for-the-public-rating-users-and-rated-items.pdf
- Four themes: Environment, Labor & Human Rights, Ethics, Sustainable Procurement. Seven management indicators per theme; theme weights set by industry, size, and country risk.
- Indicator weights within a theme: Policies 20%, Endorsements 5%, Actions 40%, Reporting 14%, 360° Watch (external stakeholder/news screening) 21%. Score adjusted by company size and country-risk factors.
- Score 0–100. Bands: Outstanding 85–100; Advanced 65–84; Good 45–64; Partial 25–44; Insufficient 0–24.
- Medals are percentile-based vs all companies rated in the prior 12 months: Platinum top 1% (99+), Gold top 5% (95+), Silver top 15% (85+), Bronze top 35% (65+). Ineligible if any theme score <30/100. "An EcoVadis Medal or Badge should not be understood as certification."
- Badges: Committed = score ≥45; Fast Mover = score 34–44 with ≥6-point gain within 18 months (https://support.ecovadis.com/hc/en-us/articles/210460227-Understanding-EcoVadis-Medals-and-Badges).
- Validity: 12 months from publication. Up to 10 years of historical data reviewed. "Absence of document evidence means absence of management approach" — self-declarations are not scored.
- Scale (https://ecovadis.com/solutions/ratings/): 150,000+ companies rated, 180 countries, 205 industries, 14 languages, EcoVadis Academy 44+ e-learning courses. 2025 Purpose Report: rated network 175,000; 25,852 new rated companies in 2025; companies returning for reassessment improved by an average of 15 points; 55,838 companies report at least one GHG metric; >1,400 procurement leaders / >$2.5T spend connected; 2030 target 300,000 rated companies (https://esgnews.com/ecovadis-2025-purpose-report-targets-300000-companies-by-2030-as-sustainable-procurement-spend-tops-2-1-trillion/ and https://esgpost.com/sustainability-intelligence-connects-2-5tn-in-global-spend-ecovadis/).
- The "21 criteria" count appears only in third-party guides (https://www.getsunhat.com/hub/ecovadis-rating). **[Not in the 2026 methodology document read.]**

### 2.2 Cost to suppliers
- EcoVadis official: "Subscription fees are tailored to each company based on: Company size (such as the number of employees); Selected subscription plan (such as Basic, Premium, Select, or Corporate); Location." Prices exclusive of VAT. No published price list. https://support.ecovadis.com/hc/en-us/articles/360011788959
- Third-party indicative annual fees (Dcycle, a consultancy): Basic ≈ €1,080; Premium ≈ €1,500; Select ≈ €4,700; Corporate ≈ €7,650; US range $500–$11,000+; quotes vary ±30–50%; internal effort "usually the largest part of total cost." https://dcycle.io/blog/ecovadis-pricing-plans-medals and https://help.dcycle.io/learning-hub/ecovadis-pricing-medals **[Third-party estimates.]**
- Tacto procurement glossary: small companies ~€500–1,500/yr; "many procurement organizations cover the costs for strategic suppliers." https://www.tacto.ai/en/procurement-glossary/ecovadis-rating **[Third-party.]**
- Buyer-side program cost: "Reported $15,000–$40,000/year for buyers assessing 50–100 suppliers," billed annually, scaling with supplier count. https://erpresearch.com/erp-add-ons/supplier-management/ecovadis/pricing **[Third-party; implies ~$150–$800 per supplier, my calc.]**

### 2.3 Completion burden and timelines
- Standard questionnaire: "20–50 questions which require company-specific documents"; Anthesis advises budgeting 6–10 weeks (6 weeks minimum for first-timers). https://anthesisgroup.com/regulations-hub/ecovadis
- EcoVadis's own framing: "a few hours to a few days to complete" (quoted via https://www.tanso.de/en/blog/ecovadis-rating); larger companies report 60–80 hours of data gathering (same source). Max 55 documents uploadable; documents must be dated >3 months before and not created solely for the questionnaire (https://help.greenly.earth/en/articles/13160606-challenges-about-ecovadis-reporting). **[Vendor sources.]**
- No EcoVadis-published SME completion-rate statistic was found. **[NOT FOUND]** Buyer-side proxies: KDDI reports ~66% of suppliers it surveyed have undergone an EcoVadis assessment (https://www.kddi.com/english/corporate/sustainability/supply-chain/01); Servier target ">90% of our top 100 suppliers assessed on CSR criteria by 2025" (https://servier.com/en/sustainability/people/sustainable-procurement/); University of Victoria 1-year pilot rated 35 suppliers (https://uvic.ca/sustainability/involved/sustainability-fund/projects/csf025-ecovadis/index.php).

### 2.4 Corrective action / improvement tools
- Every scorecard improvement area automatically generates a "Not started" corrective action on the platform (https://support.ecovadis.com/hc/tr-tr/articles/360025780871).
- Outcomes at single buyers: Solvay — 77% of assessed suppliers engaged in corrective actions and increased their score by end-2025 (https://www.solvay.com/sites/g/files/srpend616/files/2026-06/Procurement%20sustainability%20KPIs%202025.pdf); WACKER 2023 — 63% of reassessed suppliers improved (https://reports.wacker.com/2023/annual-report/non-financial-report/supply-chain/supplier-evaluation.html).
- EcoVadis Carbon Action Manager: J&J began using it in 2025 "to understand the maturity level of our largest-emitting suppliers" (https://healthforhumanityreport.jnj.com/2025/_assets/downloads/environmental-stewardship-jnj-hfh25.pdf).
- Medtech supplier-side example: Qosina (components supplier) joined EcoVadis in 2021 (https://businesswire.com/news/home/20210211005658/en/4918942/Qosina-and-EcoVadis-Set-to-Improve-Supply-Chain-Sustainability-for-Medical-Pharmaceutical-and-Beauty-Industries).

## 5. Ballard Center Social Impact Cycle and "social impact journey"

### 5.1 Social Impact Cycle (official)
Source: https://ballardcenter.byu.edu/social-impact-cycle and the Do Good. Better. Guidebook '26–27, Chapter 4 (https://ballardcenter.byu.edu/resources-section/do-good-better-guidebook ; PDF: https://ballardcenter.byu.edu/0000019e-9348-d49b-af9f-97dae63e0001/do-good-better-guidebook)
- Core principle at the center of the graphic: **"love the one"** — "even as you seek broader systemic change, your work must remain grounded in caring for the individual... Individuals are the experts of their own circumstances." Three rings: individual, community, world. The cycle "isn't linear and should be revisited and repeated as needed."
- **Phase 1 — Love the Problem:** "Engage in comprehensive research to better understand the problem and those affected by it." Key activities (Guidebook ch.4): (1) interview stakeholders and those experiencing the problem; (2) connect with organizations already working on the issue; (3) scope the issue (what, who, where); (4) map the issue — "identifying the negative consequences and contributing factors connected to your scoped issue"; (5) understand context; (6) cultivate compassion and gather insight. Objective: "...speak with those experiencing the issue, and find the root causes." Exit criterion: you can "clearly articulate the contributing factors and negative consequences... describe the ecosystem of organizations... and identify gaps in current solutions."
- **Phase 2 — Design the Change:** "Properly scope the problem and apply human-centered design principles to develop a theory of change." Guidebook: co-create interventions with affected communities; set outcome goals; small-scale iterative tests with evaluation; "plan for evaluation and determine early what data you'll need." Exit: "prototyping and testing have validated your solution, stakeholders have confirmed its feasibility and relevance, and a clear theory of change explains how your intervention will create the desired impact."
- **Phase 3 — Implement:** "Transition the proposed solution into an actionable intervention" at an "appropriately scaled iteration"; staged implementation plan; secure funding/legal structure; build infrastructure; develop partnerships; "adapt in real time." Exit: when outcomes emerge that can be analyzed.
- **Phase 4 — Evaluate:** "Measure the results of the intervention to see whether it helped alleviate the negative consequences of the issue." Evaluation "shifts away from anecdotal success stories and pursues clear, measurable outputs (what and how much you do), outcomes (changes in your social issue), and impact (the portion of the outcomes directly brought by your intervention)." Data informs "adjust, scale, continue, or conclude."
- Guidebook definitions (ch.3): Outputs = "direct products, services, or activities generated by an intervention... measure activity, not effectiveness"; Outcomes = "changes in a population" short- or long-term; Impact = "the portion of observed outcomes that can be directly attributed to an intervention... what would things look like without this intervention?" Issue map "help[s] identify leverage points where interventions can address root causes rather than symptoms." Best practices "maximize positive social change while minimizing unintended harm." Secondary research and context "reduce the risk of unintended harm"; wicked problems: "any implemented solutions are frequently met with unintended consequences." Corporate Social Responsibility is defined as "the efforts of a business to align its unique purpose and values to economic, environmental, and social imperatives, while continuing to address the financial expectations of shareholders and stakeholders."
- Guidebook chapters relevant to judging criteria: 8 Scoping the Issue, 10 Monolithic Thinking, 11 Ecosystem Mapping, 13 Interventions, 15 Theory of Change, 18 Outputs/Outcomes/Impact, 19 Outcome Measurement, 20 Impact Assessment.

## 6. TOMS Shoes: unintended consequences

- **Knowledge at Wharton, "The One-for-one Business Model: Avoiding Unintended Consequences," Feb 16, 2015:** https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/the-one-for-one-business-model-avoiding-unintended-consequences/ (also https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/?p=14712)
  - TOMS had given >35 million pairs in 60 countries since 2006; Bain Capital paid $300M for 50% in 2014.
  - Andreas Widmer (Seven Fund, gave TOMS a 2006 award, later reversed): free shoes undercut the local cobbler, irregular donation trucks disrupt small businesses, create dependency; "following the heart" is "a terrible idea" against poverty except in crises; charging recipients is an improvement.
  - Michael Matheson Miller (PovertyCure): giveaways are a "band aid" that ignore root causes (land titles, courts, capital); local manufacturing is an improvement.
  - Katherine Klein (Wharton Social Impact Initiative): separate marketing effectiveness from real-world impact; RCTs show some giveaways (bed nets) work, so generalizations are premature.
  - Garth Frazer (2008, Economic Journal): used-clothing donations contributed to the decline of sub-Saharan Africa's textile industry 1981–2000.
  - Neil Blumenthal (Warby Parker): charging recipients makes them "responsible consumers"; dependency depends on context.
  - TOMS's response: moved to local manufacturing in Haiti for donated shoes; coffee-for-water (2014). Adrien Edwards's 1.0/2.0/3.0 framework: treat symptoms (TOMS) → treat causes (Warby Parker) → restore capacity (microloans).
- **Wydick, Katz & Janet (2014), "Do in-kind transfers damage local markets? The case of TOMS shoe donations in El Salvador," Journal of Development Effectiveness 6(3):249–267:** cluster RCT of 979 households; "no statistically significant difference in market shoe purchases between treatment and control households," with point estimates slightly negative. https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/jdevef/v6y2014i3p249-267.html ; https://repository.usfca.edu/econ/4 Secondary summaries (Economist via https://kenopalo.com/2016/11): roughly one fewer locally bought pair per 20 donated; no effect on shoelessness, foot health or self-esteem; share of children agreeing "others should provide for my family's needs" rose from 66% to 79%. Follow-up: Wydick et al., "Shoeing the Children," World Bank Economic Review 2018. **[Dependency and health figures are from secondary coverage, not the paper text.]**
- Use for the case: unintended consequences of "free" help (supplier-paid vs buyer-paid assessments; dependency; crowding out local capacity), and the Klein point that the question is impact, not marketing.

## 7. Greenwashing law: FTC Green Guides, EU Empowering Consumers Directive, CSRD Omnibus

### 7.1 FTC Green Guides (16 CFR Part 260)
- Issued 1992, revised 1996, 1998, 2012; guidance (not binding rules) that the FTC enforces through Section 5 of the FTC Act. https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/topics/truth-advertising/green-guides ; https://federal-regs.com/title/16/part-260/
- §260.4 general environmental benefit claims: "unqualified general environmental benefit claims should be avoided" ("eco-friendly," "green") because marketers cannot substantiate every reasonable interpretation; qualify claims clearly and prominently. §260.5 carbon offsets: competent and reliable scientific evidence, no double-counting, disclose if reductions will not occur within two years. §260.6 certifications and seals are endorsements under the Endorsement Guides and must disclose the basis for certification. The Guides do not cover "sustainable," "organic," or "natural." Summary: https://www.foley.com/fr/insights/publications/2012/10/new-ftc-green-guides-address-environmental-claims ; final rule: https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/federal_register_notices/guides-use-environmental-marketing-claims-green-guides/greenguidesfrn.pdf
- Review opened Dec 2022 (asked about "net zero," "carbon neutral," recyclable); no revised Guides issued as of Aug 2026. https://stateimpactcenter.org/issues/federal-regulatory-policy/green-guides ; https://netzerocompare.com/policies/ftc-green-guides
- Practical rule for Merit: no "sustainable supply chain" headline until Scope 3 is measured; qualify any supplier claim by coverage (e.g., "x% of spend assessed") and avoid medals as product claims (EcoVadis itself says medals are "not a certification").

### 7.2 EU Directive (EU) 2024/825 "Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition" (EmpCo)
- Transposition deadline Mar 27, 2026; applies from Sep 27, 2026 (also to products already on shelves). https://commission.europa.eu/topics/consumers/consumer-rights-and-complaints/sustainable-consumption_en ; https://coslaw.eu/directive-eu-2024-825-generic-environmental-claims-and-practices-banned-in-the-eu-from-27-september-2026/
- New per-se prohibited practices added to UCPD Annex I (https://projetcelsius.com/en/blog/banned-environmental-claims-eu/): (2a) sustainability labels not based on a certification scheme or set by public authorities; (4a) generic environmental claims ("green," "eco-friendly," "climate friendly") unless recognised excellent environmental performance (EU Ecolabel, type-I ecolabel, or top class under EU law) can be shown; (4b) claiming a benefit for the whole product/business when it concerns only one aspect; (4c) claims that a product has a neutral, reduced or positive climate impact based on GHG offsetting; (10a) presenting legal requirements as a distinctive feature; (23d–23j) durability/repairability/obsolescence practices. Under Article 6, future-performance claims ("net zero by 2040") are misleading unless backed by clear, objective, publicly available commitments with a detailed implementation plan, measurable targets, resources, and independent third-party verification.
- Many Member States missed the transposition deadline (e.g., France as of Sep 28, 2026), so enforcement timing varies. **[Secondary legal commentary; verify in OJ text for exact wording.]**

### 7.3 CSRD / CSDDD Omnibus I (2025–26) — one paragraph
Provisional political agreement Dec 9, 2025 (reported adopted Feb 24, 2026 by some sources). CSRD scope narrows to EU companies with >1,000 employees AND >€450M net turnover, applying for financial years from Jan 1, 2027; non-EU parents are captured only with >€450M EU turnover in each of the last two years plus an EU subsidiary or branch >€200M. Estimates put remaining in-scope companies at ~5,000 (from ~50,000). A **value-chain cap** makes suppliers with up to 1,000 employees "protected undertakings": reporters may not demand data beyond the voluntary VSME standard, may rely on self-declarations, and must tell suppliers they can refuse. Sector-specific ESRS are dropped; core ESRS to be cut back; limited assurance only (standards by Jul 1, 2027). CSDDD applies only to >5,000 employees and >€1.5B turnover from Jul 26, 2029, with penalties capped at 3% of worldwide turnover and the harmonised civil-liability regime removed. For a ~$1.5B medtech, this means CSRD reporting is unlikely to apply directly; the practical effect is that EU customers' data requests to Merit and Merit's own requests to small suppliers converge on VSME-level data.
